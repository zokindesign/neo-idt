## Definition
The degree of users' concern and worry, when using a product or service, that personal information may be leaked, tracked, or misused, and that physical safety may be threatened — including feelings about data privacy, anonymity, protection from harassment, and physical safety.

## Why It Matters
Privacy and security concerns directly determine whether users are willing to adopt, trust, and continue using a product or service. Even a functionally powerful product can lead to abandonment or a switch to a competitor if users feel unsafe or that their privacy is violated. Considering privacy and security helps design products or services that are trustworthy, respect user autonomy, and reduce worry.

## Related Standards and References
- Data privacy regulations such as GDPR, CCPA, and PIPL
- ISO/IEC 27001: information security management
- Privacy by Design principles
- Security-usability research (e.g., phishing-attack prevention)
- National cybersecurity laws

## Suggested Quantification Methods
- Subjective concern scores: measuring users' level of privacy and security concern using scales (e.g., privacy concern scales)
- Behavioral indicators: whether users provide only the minimum necessary information, use anonymous mode, and the rate of consent withdrawal
- Technical implementation: degree of data encryption, adherence to the principle of least privilege, whether third-party privacy certification is obtained
- Incident records: privacy complaints, data-breach incidents, time to fix security vulnerabilities
- Data sources: user surveys, behavioral logs, security audit reports, transparency reports

## Manifestations Across Domains
- **Digital products**: data minimization collection, explicit privacy policies, opt-out of tracking, end-to-end encryption, anonymous login options
- **Industrial design**: whether the product includes anti-tamper design, whether usage data is collected and how users are told about it
- **Architectural design**: explicit signage of surveillance cameras and usage policies, transparency of access-control systems
- **Interior design**: data-usage explanations for smart-home devices, wake-word privacy modes for voice assistants
- **Graphic design**: whether advertising avoids making users feel tracked (e.g., overly targeted ads)
- **Fashion design**: whether the collection and transmission methods of smart-garment data are transparent and disableable by users

## Common Misconceptions and Blind Spots
- Assuming that users will sacrifice privacy as long as the product works well
- Considering privacy only when legally required rather than proactively building trust
- Privacy policies that are too long and full of legal jargon for users to understand
- Failing to provide enough control options when personalization features are needed
- Equating anonymity with loss of function instead of designing a controllable degree of sharing

## Design Recommendation Keywords
Data minimization, transparency, user control, anonymous options, security by design, trust building, privacy dashboard, consent management, minimum-necessary principle
