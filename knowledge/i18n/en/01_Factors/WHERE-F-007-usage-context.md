# Usage Context

## Definition

The specific situational conditions under which a product or service is used, including the user's posture (sitting, standing, walking), one-handed vs two-handed operation, whether on the move, whether attention is focused, whether other tasks are being performed simultaneously (multitasking), the degree of time pressure, and the frequency and duration of use.

## Why It Matters

Usage context directly affects users' perception, operation, and cognitive load. For example, using a phone while walking requires larger touch targets and more concise information; one-handed operation requires easily reachable controls; highly distracting situations require more salient feedback and lower cognitive load. Considering usage context helps design products and services that remain usable, safe, and efficient under real-world conditions.

## Subtypes and Specific Dimensions

### Posture and hand availability
- Sitting / standing / walking / lying / crouching; one-handed / two-handed / no-handed operation; moving / stationary; vibration / unstable platforms

### Attention and cognitive load
- Focused / distracted / multitasking; time pressure; competition for cognitive resources; situationally increased cognitive load

### Sensory environment
- Lighting (bright / dim / glare), acoustics (noisy / quiet / echoing), tactile conditions (gloves / wet hands / dirty hands)

### Social and privacy context
- Alone / in company / observed; public / semi-public / private spaces; social pressure

### Situational capability limits
> B1 cross-reference reinforcement (INCL-XREF-001, benchmarked against Microsoft Inclusive Design and WHO ICF d4/d5-d6)

**Situational limitations**: not an inherent deficit of the user, but a temporary functional restriction caused by the situation
- **Visual restriction**: screen invisible in bright light, night-driving glare, wearing sunglasses or a helmet
- **Auditory restriction**: speech inaudible in noisy environments, no audio playback allowed in quiet venues, wearing noise-cancelling headphones
- **Motor restriction**: one-handed operation (holding a child / carrying heavy loads / an injured arm / gripping a handrail), wearing gloves or a face mask, confined spaces
- **Cognitive restriction**: reduced processing capacity under pressure, distraction, multitasking, fatigue, or acute stress

**Design implication**: situational limitations overlap heavily with permanent disabilities in interaction requirements. A unified "situation-adaptation mode" can serve both groups at once.

### Mobility and travel scenarios
> B1 cross-reference reinforcement (INCL-XREF-001, benchmarked against WHO ICF d4)
- **Walking / wheelchair / crutches / walker**: device use while moving
- **Public transport** (subway / bus / airplane): space constraints, vibration, noise, standing / crowding
- **Driving / cycling**: hands-free operation, voice control, head-up displays, minimal glance time
- **Cross-modal transitions**: seamless experience across walking → subway → transfers (account / progress / settings sync)

### Private and sensitive-task scenarios
> B1 cross-reference reinforcement (INCL-XREF-001, benchmarked against WHO ICF d5-d6 self-care and domestic life)
- **Toilet / changing / bathing**: waterproofing, slip resistance, easy cleaning, privacy protection
- **Medical / medication / monitoring**: privacy, accuracy, reminders, data logging
- **Finance / legal / identity verification**: security, shoulder-surfing protection, audit trails
- **Menstruation / incontinence / stoma care**: discretion, easy access, leak prevention, freedom from embarrassment

## Suggested Quantification Methods
- Posture classification: proportions of sitting, standing, walking, lying, etc.
- Hand availability: assessment of one-handed operation feasibility (e.g., degrees of hand freedom)
- Attention load: secondary-task reaction time, subjective distraction ratings
- Time pressure: available completion time, deadline urgency scores
- Frequency and duration of use: sessions per day, average session length
- Data sources: context observation, wearable sensors, log analysis, user interviews

## Manifestations Across Domains
- **Digital products**: driving mode, one-handed interfaces, large-font options, voice control, fewer interaction steps
- **Industrial design**: one-handed tools, non-slip grips, heights usable while standing, quick interfaces
- **Architectural design**: clear signage in emergencies, handrail heights adapted to different postures, seating design in waiting areas
- **Interior design**: bedside switches within easy reach, height-zoned kitchen worktops, living-room seating with convenient side access
- **Graphic design**: fast readability of outdoor ads, concise messaging on vehicle ads, reading distances for exhibition panels
- **Fashion design**: pocket placement for easy access, easy-pull zipper design, cuts that don't restrict movement

## Common Pitfalls and Blind Spots
- Considering only the ideal, seated, focused usage situation
- Ignoring dynamic changes in usage context (e.g., transitions from sitting to walking)
- Looking only at average situations and ignoring edge or high-risk ones (e.g., emergencies)
- Not accounting for competition for cognitive resources in multitasking scenarios
- Designing for "static use" without considering movement or unstable platforms

## Design Recommendation Keywords
Situation adaptation, one-handed operation, postural freedom, attention-friendly, time flexibility, multitask-compatible, emergency salience, mobile-optimized
