## Definition
Users' preferences for, sensitivity to, and aversion to sensory stimuli (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory), including their subjective impressions of and physiological reactions to brightness, color, sound, vibration, texture, and other sensory attributes.

## Why It Matters
Sensory preferences directly affect users' comfort, acceptance, and willingness to use. For example, users sensitive to bright light may avoid high-brightness interfaces, and users averse to certain textures may refuse to use specific products. Considering sensory preferences helps design products or services that are sensory-friendly, minimize discomfort, and match users' subjective experience.

## Related Standards and References
- WCAG 2.1: guidance on color and contrast (but it does not cover subjective preferences)
- ISO 9241-210: human-centred design (covering user comfort)
- Sensory evaluation methods: e.g., sensory difference tests, preference tests
- Sensory-processing sensitivity models in neuroscience
- Industry sensory design guidelines (e.g., automotive interiors, food packaging)

## Suggested Quantification Methods
- Subjective ratings: Likert scales of liking or discomfort for specific sensory stimuli (e.g., 1-7 from strongly dislike to strongly like)
- Physiological measures: responses such as heart-rate variability, skin conductance, and pupil size
- Choice tests: asking users to choose between different sensory conditions
- Data sources: sensory lab testing, user research, focus groups

## Manifestations Across Domains
- **Digital products**: adjustable color themes, dark mode, adjustable sound feedback, vibration-strength options, reduced flashing/animation options
- **Industrial design**: handle material choices (rubber, metal, wood grain), operating-sound design, product weight and balance
- **Architectural design**: material textures (walls, handrails), control of ambient sound (echo, noise), combinations of natural and artificial light
- **Interior design**: preferences in soft-furnishing materials (fabric, leather), light color-temperature choices, scent management in spaces
- **Graphic design**: color-pairing preferences, use of texture, tactile print effects (e.g., embossing, foil stamping)
- **Fashion design**: fabric hand-feel (cotton, silk, synthetics), fit on the body, tactile comfort of labels and seams

## Common Misconceptions and Blind Spots
- Assuming one "neutral" sensory design suits everyone
- Designing to average preferences and ignoring polarized groups (users who love brightness coexist with users who loathe it)
- Ignoring that sensory preferences shift with context, fatigue, or health status
- Over-relying on vision or hearing while neglecting other sensory channels (touch, smell)

## Design Recommendation Keywords
Sensory adjustability, active feedback, material selection, sound design, tactile friendliness, low-stimulation options, personalized sensory settings, sensory consistency
