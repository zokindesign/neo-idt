## Definition
The competitive position of a product or service in its target market — including target customer segments, value proposition, differentiation advantages, pricing strategy, and brand image — which determines its appeal and competitiveness in a given segment.

## Why It Matters
Market positioning directly affects a product's commercial success and sustainability. Ignoring market positioning can leave an inclusive design usable but unable to attract enough users or generate profit, making it hard to scale. Considering market positioning helps combine inclusive features with target users' needs, willingness to pay, and brand expectations, balancing social and commercial value.

## Related Standards and References
- Porter's Five Forces model
- STP marketing theory (segmentation, targeting, positioning)
- Business Model Canvas (value propositions, customer relationships, revenue streams)
- Kano model (basic needs, performance needs, delight needs)
- Social enterprise and B Corp assessment criteria
- Social value weighting in government procurement across jurisdictions

## Suggested Quantification Methods
- Target market size: TAM (total addressable market), SAM (serviceable available market), SOM (serviceable obtainable market)
- Users' willingness to pay a premium for inclusive features (WTP surveys)
- Brand perception surveys: awareness and favorability within the target group
- Differentiation index: advantage scores over competitors on inclusive dimensions
- Revenue forecasting: financial models based on pricing strategy and market penetration rate
- Data sources: market research reports, competitor analysis, user interviews, industry benchmarks

## Manifestations Across Domains
- **Digital products**: inclusive features as paid entry points in freemium models, enterprise-level accessibility compliance as a selling point, audience coverage for advertising-supported apps
- **Industrial design**: ergonomic premium in the high-end market, cost-benefit balance in the mass market, compliance with accessibility standards in government procurement
- **Architectural design**: accessibility upscale finishes in luxury residences, statutory minimums for public buildings, cost-sharing for aging-friendly retrofits in the rental market
- **Interior design**: anthropometric fit in high-end bespoke furniture, conformance with universal design standards in the wholesale market
- **Graphic design**: readability and recognizability within brand VI systems, precise reach of target groups in public-interest advertising
- **Fashion design**: basic adaptability in mass fast fashion, individualized accessibility needs in high-end bespoke, balance between functionality and professional image for workwear

## Common Misconceptions and Blind Spots
- Seeing inclusion as belonging only to charity or compliance, ignoring its potential as an innovation and differentiation advantage
- Catering only to the most demanding standards while ignoring most users' actual acceptance and willingness to pay
- Focusing excessively on technical implementation in the early concept stage while ignoring market validation
- Failing to account for positioning differences across channels (online vs. offline, B2B vs. B2C)
- Treating inclusion as an add-on feature rather than part of the core value proposition

## Design Recommendation Keywords
